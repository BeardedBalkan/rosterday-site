How to Recruit Volunteers at Your Club's Registration Day

Registration day is the only moment all season when every family is standing in front of you. Here is how to use it to fill your volunteer roster for the year.

5 min read·For Australian football clubs

There is exactly one day a year when every family in your club walks through the door, stands in a queue, and has nothing to do for eight minutes.

Most clubs spend that eight minutes on shin pads and payment plans, and then spend the following six months sending increasingly desperate messages into a group chat asking if anyone can do the canteen.

Registration day is the highest-leverage volunteer recruitment opportunity your club gets. Here is how to actually use it.

Ask before they pay, not after

The order matters more than anything else on this page.

A family filling out a registration form is in a compliant, transactional frame of mind. They are already answering questions, ticking boxes, and providing information. One more question lands easily.

The same family, two months later, mid-season, is being interrupted. The ask is now an imposition rather than part of a process.

So put the volunteer question inside the registration flow, not in a follow-up email.

Ask a specific question, not an open one

"Are you interested in helping out?" is close to useless. It invites people to assess their own enthusiasm, and most people will honestly conclude they are not especially enthusiastic.

Replace it with a closed question that assumes participation:

Our club is run entirely by volunteers. Every family covers two match-day duties per season, about four hours in total. Which of these would you prefer?

  • Canteen
  • BBQ
  • Gate and gear
  • Ground setup or pack up
  • Team manager
  • I would rather pay the volunteer levy

Now the family is choosing between options rather than deciding whether to participate. Almost everyone picks something. And the ones who pick the levy have made a clean, guilt-free choice that funds the club, which is a perfectly good outcome.

Note the shift: "which would you prefer" produces answers. "Would you like to help" produces silence.

Capture the second name

This is the single most valuable field on your registration form and almost no club has it.

Is there anyone else in your family who might be willing to help occasionally? A grandparent, an older sibling, a family friend? Name and mobile number.

Most families will offer one or two names without hesitation. Grandad, who is at every game anyway. The 17-year-old who finished playing juniors last year. The uncle with the good tongs.

These people are already at your ground on a Saturday. They have never been asked, because your club has no way to reach anyone who is not a registered member. One field on a form doubles the size of your volunteer pool, at zero cost.

Make sure you note that the family has consented to you contacting that person, because you will be texting them later and the Spam Act applies to community clubs the same as anyone.

Have a physical presence at sign-on

If you run an in-person sign-on day, run a volunteer table next to the registration table. Not a poster. A table with a person at it.

What works on that table:

A visible roster with gaps in it. People respond to a visible shortfall far more than to an abstract request. A printed Round 1 to 4 canteen roster with four empty cells and a pen is more persuasive than any speech.

Named jobs with hours attached. "Ground setup, 7:30 to 8:30, two people needed, mostly moving goals and cones." People say no to vague commitments and yes to defined ones.

One committee member per age group. Families respond to someone who knows their kid's name. Cold recruitment from a stranger at a table converts poorly.

A QR code to the season roster. For the ones who say "let me check with my partner". Give them a link they can open that night while the conversation is still fresh.

Say what the club cannot do without volunteers

Clubs are often shy about this, which is a mistake. Families do not know how close to the line most grassroots clubs run.

A short, factual paragraph in your registration pack, without guilt-tripping:

Our club has no paid staff. The canteen, the barbecue, gate duty, ground setup, the fields being lined on a Saturday morning, and every team having a manager are all done by parents. Last season, 78 per cent of our volunteer hours were done by 14 per cent of our families. We are trying to change that this year, which is why we are asking every family for two duties.

Real numbers, stated plainly. No pleading. This lands, particularly with the middle group of families who never realised the distribution was that lopsided.

Follow up within a week, while it is warm

Whatever people committed to at registration, confirm it within seven days with something concrete. Not "thanks for volunteering", which decays into nothing. Something like:

Thanks for putting your hand up for canteen. Your team has Round 4, which is 12 April. Here are the blocks that morning, pick whichever suits.

The gap between an expression of interest and an actual rostered shift is where most club volunteer recruitment dies. Close it fast, in the week when the person still remembers agreeing.

What to do with the people who said no

Nothing punitive. Note it, respect it, and ask again next year.

Some families genuinely cannot. Shift workers, single parents, people with a second child in another sport on Saturdays, people going through things you know nothing about. A club that treats non-volunteering as a moral failure will lose members, and it will lose the wrong ones.

The levy option matters here. It gives a family that cannot contribute time a dignified way to contribute anyway, and it removes the awkwardness from every subsequent conversation.

The measure of success

The test of a good registration day is not how many people said yes on the day. It is how many rostered shifts you have filled by Round 3.

If you got 60 expressions of interest and your Round 3 canteen is still empty, the recruitment worked and the conversion failed. That gap is almost always caused by the same thing: nobody turned the interest into a specific, confirmed shift while it was still fresh.
